Here are three genuinely new research directions that push the Returning to the Living program into untested empirical territory rather than repeating existing validation steps:

1. The "Surprise Test" as a Meta-Cognitive Classifier for Inner Speech vs. Parasocial Simulation

One-line title: Testing whether conversational predictability ("lack of surprise") reliably distinguishes self-generated rumination from genuine interpersonal perspective-taking.

Why it matters: Your novel introduced a sharp, intuitive heuristic—Kesh’s question, "When did she last surprise you?"—to help people tell when an imagined dialogue with an absent or deceased person has collapsed into an echo chamber. While dialogical self theory and inner-speech research study how we simulate others' voices, no empirical study has tested whether subjective unpredictability/surprise acts as a reliable behavioral marker of healthy mentalizing versus repetitive brooding, or whether prompting people with the "surprise test" actively breaks cognitive fusion.

Concrete small study design:

Participants: 60–80 adults experiencing moderate-to-high daily rumination or unfinished interpersonal conflict (screened via the Ruminative Responses Scale, excluding acute crisis or prolonged grief disorder).

Design & Duration: A 10-day Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) micro-randomized trial. Three times daily, when participants report having an imagined conversation or replaying an interaction, they rate: (1) how vivid the other person's voice felt, (2) whether the imagined voice said anything unexpected/surprising, and (3) current distress. In 50% of episodes (randomized within-person), the prompt delivers the Surprise Test intervention ("Ask yourself: when did this imagined voice last surprise me? If never, label it as your own voice in their cadence").

Measures: Immediate post-prompt cognitive defusion (State Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire), 30-minute follow-up rumination persistence, and behavioral shift (whether they initiated a real-world interaction or task).

Main risk or ethical concern: In bereaved individuals, realizing that a comforting inner voice is "only their own mind" can trigger acute secondary grief or emotional drop-off. The protocol requires clear pre-briefing (framing the test as a tool for stuck loops, not a dismissal of cherished memories) and a mood-repair/grounding screen at the end of each prompt.

Single most important existing paper to read first: Alderson-Day, B., & Fernyhough, C. (2015). Inner speech: Development, cognitive functions, phenomenology, and neurobiology. Psychological Bulletin, 141(5), 931–965.

2. Dyadic "Read-Back" Grounding vs. Co-Rumination and Reassurance-Seeking

One-line title: Isolating the boundary conditions where structured interpersonal check-ins ("Where are you? / Here. Heard.") co-regulate distress without devolving into co-rumination or partner burnout.

Why it matters: Your unified paper identifies U5 (whether a living voice beats a solitary grounding technique) as its central open question, proposing a paired Return Card. However, clinical interpersonal emotion regulation (IER) research shows that turning to a partner during rumination frequently backfires into co-rumination (mutually rehashing the problem) or excessive reassurance-seeking, which erodes relationship satisfaction and burdens the partner. Testing a strictly constrained, content-free call-and-answer protocol against standard supportive check-ins directly tests whether removing narrative rehashing preserves the relational "key" while preventing interpersonal exhaustion.

Concrete small study design:

Participants: 40–50 adult dyads (romantic partners, close friends, or roommates) where at least one partner reports frequent worry/brooding.

Design & Duration: A 3-week randomized controlled field study comparing two dyadic communication rules when a partner notices drift or looping: (1) Constrained Call-and-Answer Arm (strictly limited to the procedural read-back "Where are you?" / "Here. Heard." plus naming one physical object in the room, with a rule against discussing the content of the worry for 15 minutes), versus (2) Unconstrained Supportive Check-In Arm (partners check in and talk through whatever is on their mind).

Measures: Pre/post brooding (RRS), daily co-rumination frequency, Perceived Partner Responsiveness Scale (Reis et al., 2004), and a daily Partner Burden & Resentment scale completed by the responding partner.

Main risk or ethical concern: Relational asymmetry and emotional labor. If one partner treats the prompt as an on-demand emotional regulation button, the responding partner may feel policed or fatigued. The study must cap daily initiated calls (e.g., max 2 per day) and explicitly protect the partner's right to decline or delay response.

Single most important existing paper to read first: Zaki, J., & Williams, W. C. (2013). Interpersonal emotion regulation. Emotion, 13(5), 803–810 (alongside Rose, A. J., 2002, on co-rumination).

Which One I Would Pick First—and Why

I would pick Direction #2 (Dyadic "Read-Back" Grounding vs. Co-Rumination and Reassurance-Seeking).

It tests the load-bearing beam of your entire synthesis: Your unified paper stakes its core claim on U5—that a living voice pulls someone out of an inner loop more reliably than a solitary technique—and explicitly identifies the paired Return Card as the next step.

It directly prevents the single biggest real-world failure mode: In clinical and everyday relationships, telling people to "use a living person as your key out of rumination" without strict conversational guardrails reliably degenerates into co-rumination or reassurance-seeking, burning out the very relationships the framework is trying to protect. Proving that a content-free procedural read-back ("Here. Heard.") breaks the loop without burdening the partner would give your framework an actionable, empirically defensible mechanism that therapists, app designers, and everyday couples could immediately use.

It is fast, cheap, and decisively falsifiable: Unlike building and moderating a multi-week token economy (Direction #3), a 3-week dyadic text/voice check-in study can be run by an independent researcher using free survey/EMA tools, produces clean quantitative data on both partners, and directly fulfills your paper's closing mandate: no more theory until there is measurement.
