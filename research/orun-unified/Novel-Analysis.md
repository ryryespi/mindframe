## 1. The book at a glance

**Length and shape.** About 29,400 words in twenty chapters, plus an opening section on Orun and an afterword, in four parts:

| Part | Chapters | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| One: The Morning She Tried to Buy | I–V | Neru tries to buy, sew, freeze, or bargain her mother back and her sister home. Every route fails. |
| Two: In Her Name | VI–X | She opens Alun's rivel correctly, falls into a loop of imagined talk, learns the Drop, and goes downriver to repair things with Vethan. |
| Three: The Wet Year | XI–XVI | Outside pressure arrives: a stranger, a dispute over Alun's page, the river moving, and gossip. Neru becomes "the keeper" and relapses. Ilun breaks her silence. |
| Four: The Day of Enough | XVII–XX | Vethan comes by choice. The high water forces the town to call and answer. Living names are added to the work. Vethan leaves by choice, and Neru receives the fixed morning gift as enough. |

**Through-line.** A grieving woman learns the difference between memory and relationship. The book's central image moves from a hill of glass hidden under a bowl (Chapter I) to a single measure of glass held in an open hand and given away (Chapter XX).

## 2. Themes

1. **Reality is relational.** The book's strongest claim, and the one Copilot named first in its co-author notes. People are pulled back into the present by other people: a sandal on stone (I), a boy with a cup (II, VIII), Sareth's thumb on her palm (III), Kesh's wrist (IV), gardeners arguing (XV), Ilun's sentence (XVI), a call across water (XVIII). "Relationships snap you back in reality" is said aloud only in the sayings and once near the end. Everywhere else it is shown.
2. **Grief is lived with, not solved.** Alun never returns. Vethan does not move home. Ren and Sareth sit with "a space between them the width of a child." Rivel keeps a person's influence without bringing them back.
3. **Communication is repair, not victory.** No villain causes the misunderstandings. The sisters' worst wound is a carefully written sentence ("I'm not asking") that reads as not caring (XVII). The cure is the slow, embarrassing work of saying what you meant and hearing what landed.
4. **Keeping a voice versus freezing it.** New in the novel, from Copilot's notes. Neru guards Alun's firing page, and Kesh sells his grandfather's unwalked maps. Both are preserving the ink and losing the walking. A barge grounds on a bar no map shows (XIV).
5. **Attention to the dead versus the living.** Tulen asks the book's sharpest question: "If everybody's attention goes to the rack, who's listening to us?" (XII, XIX). "Attention is life" turns out to cut both ways.
6. **Giving and receiving.** "I receive it" is an honored answer. Bram, raised in a lending town, has to learn to receive bread without signing for it (XI) before he can understand why rivel cannot be sold (XIX).
7. **Choice and consent.** Coming home, leaving, opening rivel, joining the circle, and walking nearer the rack are all choices that no one else can make for you. The book's emotional climax is not a homecoming. It is Vethan putting her own cup away so that it can be a cup (XX).

## 3. Character arcs

| Character | Starts | Turns | Ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neru** | Tries to buy the morning back (I) | Learns the Drop (VIII), then becomes "the keeper" and uses Alun's voice to win an argument (XII). Ilun breaks her silence (XVI). She drops the map and answers the call (XVIII). | Gives up the lamp (XIX), keeps the felt love while the exact voice fades (XX), and gives her glass away |
| **Vethan** | "The sister who left" (II, V) | Gets a life of her own: the dike, the calling, Ila (XIII) | Saves people with what she learned elsewhere (XVIII), changes Orun (XIX), and leaves by choice (XX) |
| **Kesh** | Offers the false ledger (IV) | Refuses to copy more of Alun's pages for Neru (VII). Is shamed by gossip (XIV) and moves from shame to guilt to repair. | Walks the fords every week, consents to his grandfather's rivel, puts his own mark beside the old man's, and stops talking to the dead at the fords (XIX, XX) |
| **Ilun** | A silence others can put anything into (V) | Her silence becomes Neru's loop (XVI). She tells the story of her brother and the sentence she can never take back. | Speaks with purpose; answers first, "like a bell," in the rescue (XVIII) |
| **Sareth** | Can't tell keeping a song from hoarding it (II) | Lets the song go into children's mouths (VIII) | Takes the keeper's lamp and promises to "say the names, all of them, the living too" (XIX); Ren comes, by his own choice (XVIII) |
| **Tulen** | A boy with a cup (II) | Asks who is listening to the living (XII). Is asked, at last, what he saw from the raft (XVIII). | His name is written on the ford map (XX) |
| **Tamar** | Reads a dead woman's page with her lips moving (X) | Challenges the keeper with cracked cups (XII) | Her true mark goes into the margin in her own name (XIX) |
| **Bram** | Owner logic: "if you don't own a thing, someone else will" (XI) | Names Neru's slip on the steps (XII). Offers a clean list of sayings that the hall hands back (XVI). | Will carry the method "in his hands," say both names, and refuse to sell (XIX) |

**Notes on balance.** Copilot was right that Vethan was thin. The novel's best structural decision was to give her expertise from elsewhere that Orun needs. The weakest supporting arc is Ren. He appears late and says one line, by design (he comes only by choice), but readers may want more. Mira is comic relief with one serious beat, about her aunt's bread and half the salt. It works.

## 4. How the research is dramatized

### 4.1 C-R-Z pillars

| Pillar (paper's grade) | Dramatization | Strength of fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Calibration:** notice and interrupt. Supported as a generic motor or attention interrupt; the ASL "C" mechanism is speculative. | Neru learns the signs of the loop (VIII), then uses them in the hall (XIV), in luoma (XV), and on the stair (XVIII). There is no handshape. | Strong for noticing. The novel never tests the ASL cue, wisely. |
| **Reality-Return:** outward re-engagement. Supported as a family of grounding practices. | The search for "the key that belongs to now," and the refinement "not the first sound" (XV) | Very strong. This is the novel's spine. |
| **Zero-Velocity:** weight into the ground. Plantar input is supported; literal *v* = 0 is speculative. | "Put her weight down into her feet... feeling for the bottom" (XV); "put her feet down hard on the wet stone" (XVIII) | Good, and modest. The image is the shallows, not zero. |
| **Drop-Press-Snap** as a package. Plausible, untested. | The Drop in XV follows the C-R-Z order: shoulders, feet, let go, listen. | Good. The novel adds its own refinement: the key is often a person. |

### 4.2 Attention as Selu (C-R-Z Section 5)

- **5.1, switching is costly:** Neru can't be both at the rack and in the hall. When the keeper's role takes her attention, Tulen goes unheard (XII).
- **5.2, the Drop as letting go:** "Not the way you drop a cup, by accident. The way you set down a load" (XV).
- **5.3, syncing back:** the calling is a public, structured version of repair. "Heard" is a read-back (XVIII).
- **5.4, anchors:** the river; the gardeners; the work song Vethan sings "where the hay is stubborn"; the west door as a threshold.
- **5.5, the budget:** Neru spends real glass every night at the copy-table and buys only her own voice (VII).
- **5.6, "Take heart":** present only as the dedication's faith. The novel deliberately keeps religious language out of Orun's mouths.

### 4.3 The Connection Postulate, P1–P7

| Sub-postulate (paper's grade) | How it is dramatized | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **P1.** Purpose cycles until connection completes it. *Plausible; "only when" is speculative.* | Neru's errand ("understand one thing, then I'll stop") loops through VI, VII, XII, XV, and XVI. It ends only when she answers a living call (XVIII). | Dramatized strongly, perhaps too strongly. The novel also includes a fair counterexample: Tamar completes her purpose (the new clay) mostly alone, with her hands. That keeps the "only when" clause honest. |
| **P2.** Fantasy both connects and traps. *Supported.* | The voice-cloth is "a good likeness of a voice and a poor likeness of a person" (II). Kesh's night table: "one conversation and another" (VII). Vethan wins imaginary arguments all winter (XIII). | Excellent. The book shows both halves. The rivel system connects the town, and the imagined voice traps Neru. |
| **P3.** The way out is honoring people outside the mind. *Supported that relationships matter; "only way" is [Values].* | Ilun: "I am the living person in this room" (XVI). | The single best dramatization in the book. |
| **P4.** Keep checking reality against shame, guilt, and false gossip. *Supported for negativity bias and the shame–guilt distinction; plausible as a toolkit.* | Chapter XIV. The gossip about Kesh is built on a true seed (he did ask), and Neru is tempted to confirm it in order to offload her own shame. She drops the sentence, states what she knows and what she doesn't, and later separates shame ("the kind of man") from guilt ("a thing can be mended"). | Very strong, and faithful to Dunbar's point that gossip is not simply false. The chapter is close to a dramatized "check the facts" skill. |
| **P5.** Connection takes priority, with bounds. *Supported for undivided presence; priority is [Values].* | "Heard!" (XVIII). The bounds appear too: Neru drops the sentence in the hall rather than abandoning the room (XIV). Kesh's "Don't rush me" is honored for the whole book. | Strong. The book models a priority that still respects consent and pace. |
| **P6.** When memories fade, love remains. *Supported that feelings can outlast memory of their cause; "only love remains" is [Values].* | Neru on the terrace: the exact words are gone, and "the plain, certain, bodily sense of having been loved" remains (XX). | Moving, and it strains (see Section 5.3). |
| **P7.** Being present to respond beats seeking more knowledge. *Plausible as a priority; kairos is [Values].* | Neru "did not want another page," and then answers Tulen's call: "Heard! Coming!" (XX) | Strong, and balanced by the rivel system. Knowledge still matters, but it is held in common and used by the living, not hoarded by the bereaved. |

## 5. Where the fiction strains against the research

1. **The Drop works too reliably.** In fiction, every honest Drop eventually finds a key. In life, a person may drop everything, listen, and hear nothing that helps, especially in depression, trauma, or prolonged grief. The C-R-Z paper grades the package *plausible, untested*. Chapter XVI partly corrects this: the skill used alone becomes the loop, and a person has to step in. Still, a reader could take away "technique fixes grief." **Recommendation:** the afterword or the companion should say plainly that the Drop is a coping skill, not a treatment.
2. **No one seeks care.** Orun has luoma attendants and listeners, but no character sees anything like a clinician. That is consistent with the invented world, but it means the 988 notice and the safety sections live only outside the story. The research papers handle this. The novel cannot without breaking its frame. This tension should stay visible.
3. **P6 is shown in its kindest direction only.** Feinstein, Duff, and Tranel (2010) found that sadness, as well as happiness, persisted after memory of the cause was lost. The novel lets Neru feel fear and then lands on love. That is a legitimate artistic and faith choice, labelled as Ryan's conviction, but it is not what the nearest study shows in full.
4. **P1's exclusivity is confirmed by authorial choice.** Because the author decides how loops end, the story can make connection the only exit every time. Tamar's solitary success is the novel's one counterweight. The research grade, *speculative* for "only when," should not be upgraded on the strength of the story.
5. **The rescue resolves on a technique.** Chapter XVIII risks implying that a psychological skill solves a physical emergency. The text guards against this: what saves people is rope, posts, a remembered saying, local expertise, and many hands. Neru's Drop only lets her join them. That is the right emphasis, and it should be read that way.
6. **The surprise test is untested.** Kesh's question, "When did she last surprise you?", is one of the most useful ideas in the book, and there is no research on it in this program. It is labelled *speculative* in the companion.
7. **The economy works because it is small and fictional.** Orun has no free riders, no counterfeiters, and no gaming of the morning roll, apart from Bram's outsider logic. Ostrom's commons survive because of monitoring and graduated sanctions, which the novel barely shows. Demurrage's real-world record is short and confounded.
8. **Direct truth can wound.** Ilun's "She is dead, Neru" works because of years of trust and a clear, present need. In real grief support, blunt confrontation without that relationship can do harm. The novel earns the line. A reader should not generalize it.
9. **Living credit could drift toward ranking.** Saying living names aloud is a good fix for attention to the dead. If the names began to accumulate status, though, they would recreate the hierarchy Orun forbids ("They will not rank two people in worth"). The hall's refusal of a "purse for the living" guards against the obvious version, not the subtle one.

## 6. Copilot's contributions compared with ours, honestly

### 6.1 What Copilot contributed

- **Five draft scenes**, each about 760–880 words, that seeded Chapters VII, XI, XIII, XV, and XVII. Those five chapters total about 8,000 words, or 27% of the novel. Much of their dialogue keeps Copilot's lines nearly word for word. The narration and setting were rewritten.
- **A co-author memo** whose diagnoses drove most of Parts Three and Four: low outside stakes, untested rivel, a thin Vethan, the risk of Ilun becoming a symbol, Neru becoming too important, a stranger kindness can't solve, a crisis in communication, and attention to the dead versus the living. It also proposed an act structure and an ending: "Not everyone stays... connection without possession."

### 6.2 Where Copilot was better

- **Dialogue lines that land.** "You're buying one conversation and having another." "When did the conversation last surprise you?" "People running away don't tell everyone where they're going." "As answers go, that's a real one." "I always won the arguments." "Mostly imaginary versions of everyone." The gardeners arguing about beans. Several of the book's most quotable lines are Copilot's.
- **Structural diagnosis.** Its notes were sharper about plot than our earlier drafts. Our nine-chapter story had beautiful scenes and almost no outside pressure, and Copilot said so.
- **Comedy and lightness.** The thrown loaf, "Kesh talks too much every morning," and "Oh, we're doing this already" give the book air it needed.

### 6.3 Where Copilot was weaker

- **Canon drift.** It moved Sareth downriver and made Ilun a man. It had Ilun speak in the luoma scene, which would have spent the power of Chapter XVI early. It used wage language ("Depends how useful you are") and a guest-room economy that edged toward hiring.
- **Generic texture.** Copilot's settings were fountains, market tents, merchants, and "ledgers." Ours are kiln-smoke, blue-leaf tea, river-glass, and the unbarred west door.
- **Saying the theme.** Copilot often named the point: "Communication. There it was." "Relationships snap you back into reality." "Attention was life." We cut most of these. A few remain on purpose, spoken by characters.
- **Typography as rhythm.** One-line paragraphs ("Silence. The river. A distant bell.") read well on a screen and thin out over a novel. We merged them.

### 6.4 Where we were weaker

- **Slower and more inward.** Our original chapters (I–X) carry long sentences and dense interior narration. They are strong at the sentence level and slow at the scene level.
- **Recurring phrases.** "Needing her now," "a doorway was only a doorway," and "not close and not far" recur often. Some of that is intentional refrain, and some is habit.
- **Our own preachiness.** The traveler's list (now Bram's, XVI) was ours, and it is the most sermon-like passage in the book. The novel survives it only because Kesh pushes back.

### 6.5 Net judgment

The collaboration worked because the two drafts failed in opposite directions. Copilot brought pace, plot, and quotable dialogue. We brought world consistency, ethical rules, sensory specificity, and restraint. The best chapters (VII, XIV, XVI, XVIII) mix both. Copilot is credited in the front matter, the afterword, and here.

## 7. Suggested revisions for a second edition

1. Give Ren one more scene, perhaps with Sareth at the monthly evening for the living.
2. Show one small failure of Orun's economy, such as someone gaming the morning roll, and how a hearing handles it, to answer the Ostrom gap.
3. Add a short scene in which a luoma attendant suggests that someone needs more help than the town can give, keeping the frame without a modern clinic.
4. Trim about 10% of the recurring phrases.
5. Consider giving the surprise test to a second character, so it reads as a shared folk skill rather than Kesh's insight alone.
